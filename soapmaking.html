Soapmaking

Understand the bar before you make it.

Cold and hot process soap, taught from the chemistry up: why lye must be respected, what superfat really does, how the gel phase changes a bar, and why curing is not optional.

Before anything else: lye

  • Always add lye to cold liquid, never liquid to lye.
  • Goggles, nitrile gloves, long sleeves, ventilation.
  • Skin contact: running water for 15+ minutes. Eyes: 20–30 minutes, then medical help.
  • Never change a calculated lye amount. Run every recipe through a lye calculator.

Two processes, one reaction

Soap is what happens when oils meet an alkali. Sodium hydroxide turns fats into soap within 24–48 hours. What differs is where it finishes.

Cold process soap is blended to trace and saponifies in the mold. You choose whether it goes through gel, and it needs a full cure of at least 4–6 weeks. Hot process soap is cooked on low heat until it looks like waxy mashed potatoes. Superfat oils, delicate scents, milk and honey go in after the cook, and the bars need only 1–2 weeks of hardening.

What each oil brings

Every oil changes the bar through its fatty acids. The book's balanced bars are roughly 28–30% coconut, 15–25% butters, 44–47% olive and 2–5% castor.

The gel phase

Fresh soap heats itself. In gel phase it reaches 54–82 °C and turns semi-translucent, giving brighter colors and a harder bar. Insulate to encourage it; chill to prevent it. Milk soaps and layered designs are made without gel.

Before you make it

Wear full protective gear and confirm the lye amount in a lye calculator for your exact oils. The book itself recommends SoapCalc.net.

Soapmaking questions, answered

Soapmaking

How do I handle lye safely?

Always add lye slowly to cold liquid, never the reverse. Wear full protective gear and never change a calculated lye amount.

What is saponification?

It's the reaction between oils and lye that turns them into soap. As the book says: there is no soap without lye.

Soapmaking

What's the difference between cold process and hot process soap?

Cold process soap saponifies in the mold and needs a full cure. Hot process soap is cooked until saponification is nearly complete, so it only needs 1–2 weeks to harden.

What is gel phase in soapmaking?

It's a self-heating stage where fresh soap reaches 54–82 °C and turns semi-translucent, which speeds saponification.

Soapmaking

Why is there a translucent ring in the middle of my soap?

That's a partial gel: the center heated through gel phase but the edges didn't. It's cosmetic only; extend the cure.

Soapmaking

How long should soap cure, and why?

Most balanced cold process bars need 4–6 weeks; soft-oil bars need longer, and 100% olive Castile 6–12 months. Curing lets water evaporate, the pH settle and the bar harden.

Soapmaking

What is superfat?

A deliberate excess of oil that the lye can't saponify. It stays in the bar as free oil, making it milder and ensuring no free lye remains.

What is dual superfatting?

It's the book's signature technique: a standard superfat built into the lye calculation, plus a small amount of a precious oil added after trace.

Soapmaking

What is trace?

Trace is the point where the emulsified soap batter thickens. The book works with three stages: just emulsified, thin trace and medium trace.

Soapmaking

Why did my soap accelerate?

Certain ingredients and conditions speed up trace: some fragrance oils, citrus essential oils, sugars, milks, honey, juices, a water discount and high-coconut recipes.

Soapmaking

Why did my soap seize or turn lumpy (riced)?

Usually a fragrance oil that isn't safe for cold process. Mixing fragrance and essential oils can do it too.

Soapmaking

What's the difference between essential oils and fragrance oils?

Essential oils are natural volatile plant compounds; fragrance oils are lab-made blends with a stronger, more stable scent. Both work, but fragrance oils must be cold-process safe for soap.

What happens if I use too much fragrance?

The books set clear limits but don't describe what happens above them, so stay within the stated ranges.

Soapmaking

Why did my vanilla or chocolate-scented soap turn brown?

Vanillin in the fragrance oil oxidizes in alkaline soap and turns it from beige to deep brown. A vanilla stabilizer slows or prevents it.

Soapmaking

Why did my soap develop a white coating?

That's soda ash: a harmless, powdery film of sodium carbonate formed by carbon dioxide in the air. Spray fresh soap with alcohol to prevent it.

How do different oils affect the final soap?

Each oil's fatty acids shape the bar: coconut brings hardness and big lather, olive brings mildness and conditioning, castor stabilizes lather, and butters add hardness and creaminess.

How do I read soap calculator values?

What is a water discount?

Using less water than the usual 33–38% of oil weight. The book's recipes mostly use 26–27%.

Soapmaking

Can I use milk in soap?

Yes. Milk makes a very mild, creamy bar, but its sugars and proteins scorch easily, so it must be kept cold and soaped without gel.

How do I work with honey and sugar in soap?

Both boost lather, but they heat the batter and speed up trace, so add them carefully and keep things cool.

How do I color soap naturally?

With clays, activated charcoal, spirulina, spices and macerated oils, always pre-dispersed first to avoid specks.

How do I make clean layers in soap?

Blend only to 'just emulsified', divide straight away, finish each layer separately, and keep the soap cool so it doesn't gel.

Why is my hot process soap crumbly?

It was overcooked and lost moisture. Knead in warm water, milk or aloe juice a teaspoon at a time until it's like putty.

Soapmaking

Why does Castile soap take so long?

Olive oil is mostly oleic acid, which saponifies slowly: slow trace, a soft young bar, and a 6–12 month cure.

Soapmaking

Is 100% coconut oil soap drying?

It can be. Coconut oil cleanses strongly, so as a skin bar it needs a high superfat of 15–20%. At 0–3% it's a household bar.

The Art of Personal Soapmaking

Soapmaking

Why add juice or hydrosol to the oils instead of the lye water?

Concentrated lye would react violently with acidic juice and destroy a hydrosol's compounds. In the oils they disperse and are buffered.

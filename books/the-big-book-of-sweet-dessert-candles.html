The Big Book of Sweet Dessert Candles

For a Delectable, Aromatic & Cozy Home

Waxes, wicks and temperatures first. Then cakes, cocktails and botanicals.

A practical guide to dessert-inspired candles from natural waxes: cakes, cupcakes, cocktails topped with piped 'whipped cream', and sculpted botanical pillars. It begins with the essentials: coconut, soy and beeswax, container versus mold wax, wick testing, dyes, gel and exact temperatures. Step-by-step projects with gram weights follow, along with fixes for wet spots, uneven tops and tunneling. A closing section covers essential-oil notes, solid and oil perfumes, reed diffusers, car fragrance and a massage candle.

Why is my candle tunneling?

The wick is usually too small for the container, so the melt pool never reaches the edges. Short burns make it worse.

Why does my candle have wet spots?

Temperature changes as the wax sets: a cold room, a hot pour, a cold or thick-bottomed glass, drafts, or candles too close together.

Match the wick to the container's diameter and your wax, then confirm it with a burn test. The book calls this one of the hardest parts of candle making.

Frequently asked questions

What's the difference between container wax and pillar wax?

Container wax melts lower (about 43–47 °C) and clings to glass. Mold (pillar) wax melts at about 57–63 °C, is harder and smoother, and holds its shape as it burns.

Each wax is designed for its job. A mold candle cast from container wax will, in the book's words, turn into 'a pool of oil on your table' when burned. Mold wax used in a jar causes wet spots because it doesn't adhere to the glass.

The book's main wax is coconut wax, about 80% coconut oil and its derivatives and 20% other vegetable oils, available in both container and mold grades.

Source: The Big Book of Sweet Dessert Candles — Why Different Waxes Are Needed for Container and Mold Candles · Open the article

How much fragrance should I use in a candle?

10–12% of the wax weight. For example, 19–22.8 g of fragrance in 180–185 g of coconut wax.

Check the fragrance maker's instructions for the addition temperature. The book gives two options: 55–60 °C, or a higher 73–85 °C if the supplier says so. After adding it, stir slowly clockwise for 2–3 minutes without whipping in air. In several projects the fragrance goes in just as the wax at 55–59 °C starts to turn slightly cloudy.

The cure matters too: 7–10 days lets the fragrance bind with the coconut wax and develop both cold and hot throw.

Source: The Big Book of Sweet Dessert Candles — Coconut Wax · Decorative Container Candle · Open the article

How should I prepare and burn a new candle?

Let it set 24 hours, trim the wick to 0.5 cm, cure 7–10 days, then burn 1.5–2 hours at a time until the whole surface melts, never more than 3 hours.

Container candles need 7–10 days before the first burn so the fragrance binds with the wax. Before every burn, trim and wipe the wick so burnt bits don't fall into the melt pool. For long sessions, put the candle out for 5–10 minutes every couple of hours. Never blow it out, or the smoky wick smell will mix with the fragrance next time. Use the lid or a snuffer.

Source: The Big Book of Sweet Dessert Candles — How to Burn a Candle Correctly · Open the article

Why is the top of my candle uneven or cracked?

Most often the pouring temperature. Vessel shape, fragrance amount and pouring speed also play a part. A heat gun fixes it after 24 hours.

Wait 24 hours, then re-melt the surface with a heat gun held about 15 cm away at an angle, and poke into any cracks so the voids fill. A hair dryer is only a temporary substitute: it blows too much air, the wax 'remembers' the ripples it makes, and it's slow.

Source: The Big Book of Sweet Dessert Candles — Uneven Top After Pouring a Candle? · Open the article

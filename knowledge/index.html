Every answer, one place.

67 questions answered from the books, from lye safety to wick sizing. Each article links to the chapter where the book goes deeper.

Getting Started

  • Where should I start as a complete beginner?Start with safety, then choose one craft and read its foundations before your first project.
  • What equipment do I need?A few precise tools: an accurate scale, a thermometer and the right protective gear matter most.

Safety

  • What are the key safety rules for candle making?Melt wax only in a double boiler, never over an open flame, never let it exceed 85 °C, and smother any wax fire with baking soda, never water.
  • What safety precautions do the books require?The books treat safety as non-negotiable: full protective gear, a zoned and disinfected workspace, good ventilation and an emergency plan.
  • How do I sterilize my tools and containers?Follow the four-step sterilization chain: wash, rinse, disinfect or sterilize, then sanitize containers just before filling.
  • Do I need to patch test homemade products?Always. Test every new product on the inner elbow or forearm for 24–48 hours before first use.

Soapmaking

  • How do I handle lye safely?Always add lye slowly to cold liquid, never the reverse. Wear full protective gear and never change a calculated lye amount.
  • What is saponification?It's the reaction between oils and lye that turns them into soap. As the book says: there is no soap without lye.
  • What's the difference between cold process and hot process soap?Cold process soap saponifies in the mold and needs a full cure. Hot process soap is cooked until saponification is nearly complete, so it only needs 1–2 weeks to harden.
  • What is gel phase in soapmaking?It's a self-heating stage where fresh soap reaches 54–82 °C and turns semi-translucent, which speeds saponification.
  • Why is there a translucent ring in the middle of my soap?That's a partial gel: the center heated through gel phase but the edges didn't. It's cosmetic only; extend the cure.
  • How long should soap cure, and why?Most balanced cold process bars need 4–6 weeks; soft-oil bars need longer, and 100% olive Castile 6–12 months. Curing lets water evaporate, the pH settle and the bar harden.
  • What is superfat?A deliberate excess of oil that the lye can't saponify. It stays in the bar as free oil, making it milder and ensuring no free lye remains.
  • What is dual superfatting?It's the book's signature technique: a standard superfat built into the lye calculation, plus a small amount of a precious oil added after trace.
  • What is trace?Trace is the point where the emulsified soap batter thickens. The book works with three stages: just emulsified, thin trace and medium trace.
  • Why did my soap accelerate?Certain ingredients and conditions speed up trace: some fragrance oils, citrus essential oils, sugars, milks, honey, juices, a water discount and high-coconut recipes.
  • Why did my soap seize or turn lumpy (riced)?Usually a fragrance oil that isn't safe for cold process. Mixing fragrance and essential oils can do it too.
  • What's the difference between essential oils and fragrance oils?Essential oils are natural volatile plant compounds; fragrance oils are lab-made blends with a stronger, more stable scent. Both work, but fragrance oils must be cold-process safe for soap.
  • What happens if I use too much fragrance?The books set clear limits but don't describe what happens above them, so stay within the stated ranges.
  • Why did my vanilla or chocolate-scented soap turn brown?Vanillin in the fragrance oil oxidizes in alkaline soap and turns it from beige to deep brown. A vanilla stabilizer slows or prevents it.
  • Why did my soap develop a white coating?That's soda ash: a harmless, powdery film of sodium carbonate formed by carbon dioxide in the air. Spray fresh soap with alcohol to prevent it.
  • How do different oils affect the final soap?Each oil's fatty acids shape the bar: coconut brings hardness and big lather, olive brings mildness and conditioning, castor stabilizes lather, and butters add hardness and creaminess.
  • How do I read soap calculator values?They're predictions calculated from fatty-acid profiles. Use them as a guide, not as laws.
  • What is a water discount?Using less water than the usual 33–38% of oil weight. The book's recipes mostly use 26–27%.
  • Can I use milk in soap?Yes. Milk makes a very mild, creamy bar, but its sugars and proteins scorch easily, so it must be kept cold and soaped without gel.
  • How do I work with honey and sugar in soap?Both boost lather, but they heat the batter and speed up trace, so add them carefully and keep things cool.
  • How do I color soap naturally?With clays, activated charcoal, spirulina, spices and macerated oils, always pre-dispersed first to avoid specks.
  • How do I make clean layers in soap?Blend only to 'just emulsified', divide straight away, finish each layer separately, and keep the soap cool so it doesn't gel.
  • Why is my hot process soap crumbly?It was overcooked and lost moisture. Knead in warm water, milk or aloe juice a teaspoon at a time until it's like putty.
  • Why does Castile soap take so long?Olive oil is mostly oleic acid, which saponifies slowly: slow trace, a soft young bar, and a 6–12 month cure.
  • Is 100% coconut oil soap drying?It can be. Coconut oil cleanses strongly, so as a skin bar it needs a high superfat of 15–20%. At 0–3% it's a household bar.
  • Why add juice or hydrosol to the oils instead of the lye water?Concentrated lye would react violently with acidic juice and destroy a hydrosol's compounds. In the oils they disperse and are buffered.

Natural Skincare

  • Why should facial oil go on damp skin?On damp skin, the oil forms a micro-emulsion that spreads evenly, absorbs better and doesn't leave a greasy film.
  • How much essential oil is safe in face products?No more than 1% in total, or 0.3 g in a 30 g product. Avoid phototoxic citrus oils in daytime face care.
  • Why is my body butter grainy?Shea and cocoa butter formed large, unstable crystals as they cooled. Controlled cooling and longer whipping prevent it.
  • Why is copper peptide serum blue, and what if it turns green?GHK-Cu is naturally sapphire blue. Green or brown means it may have oxidized and lost stability.
  • Why does an exfoliating toner need such a low pH?Salicylic acid only exfoliates below pH 4.5, so the toner is set at 3.8–4.2. Lactic acid and Azeclair also work better in acidic conditions.
  • How long do homemade skincare products last?From the book: cleanser, creams and toner 9 months; peptide serum and eye gel 6 months; facial oil 9–12 months; cleansing oil and shower gel 12 months; butters, scrubs and bath salts 6 months unopened, 3 months opened.
  • Why do the body scrubs contain Polysorbate 80?It emulsifies the oils with water on the skin, so the scrub rinses off as a milky emulsion instead of leaving a greasy film.
  • Why are my bath salts fizzing or clumping in the jar?They've been exposed to moisture. Make and store them completely dry.
  • How do I double cleanse with a cleansing oil?Massage the oil onto dry skin with dry hands, add water so it turns milky, rinse, then follow with a mild foam cleanser.

Formulation

  • How should I approach formulation as a beginner?Learn the protocol, then learn what each ingredient does and why it goes in at a particular phase and temperature, before you start changing recipes.
  • Why is pH important in skincare?Healthy skin's acid mantle sits at about pH 4.5–5.5. Products in that range protect the barrier, and pH also decides whether some actives and preservatives work.
  • How do I adjust the pH of a formula?Drop by drop, with a 10% acid solution to lower it or a 10% sodium hydroxide solution to raise it, checking after each addition.
  • What is an emulsion, and how do I make a cream?A cream is an oil-in-water emulsion: oil droplets held evenly through a water phase by an emulsifier. Both phases are heated to the same temperature and combined under high shear.
  • What are the phases in a formula?Groups of ingredients handled together at the same stage: water phase, oil phase, actives, preservative and pH adjustment.
  • What's the difference between a serum and a cream?The books don't define the terms side by side, but their formulas show the difference: the serum is a water-based gel, while the creams are emulsions of oil and water.
  • What are active ingredients, and when do I add them?Actives are the ingredients chosen for a specific effect, such as panthenol, peptides or niacinamide. Most are heat-sensitive, so they go into the cooling product at 38–40 °C.
  • Do I need a preservative?Any product containing water does. Anhydrous products (oils, butters, scrubs, bath salts) don't, as long as water never gets in.
  • How do I make a gel without lumps?Pre-disperse natural gums in glycerin before adding them to water, and sprinkle polymer powders slowly while stirring.

Ingredients

  • What is the purpose of a hydrosol?Hydrosols replace some or all of the water in a formula and bring their own properties: soothing, toning, astringent or refreshing.
  • How do I stop oils going rancid?Use stable oils, add an antioxidant like vitamin E or rosemary CO2 extract, and store products cool and dark.
  • How do I choose a carrier oil?By what you need it to do. For the face, the books use light, fast-absorbing oils; richer butters belong in body care.
  • What is a macerated oil?An oil in which dried botanicals have been steeped so it takes up their fat-soluble compounds, like calendula-infused oil.

Candle Making

  • Why is my candle tunneling?The wick is usually too small for the container, so the melt pool never reaches the edges. Short burns make it worse.
  • How do I choose a candle wick?Match the wick to the container's diameter and your wax, then confirm it with a burn test. The book calls this one of the hardest parts of candle making.
  • How do I test a wick?Cure the test candle 7 days, light it and time the melt pool: it should reach the edges in 1.5–2 hours at about 1 cm deep.
  • Why does my candle have wet spots?Temperature changes as the wax sets: a cold room, a hot pour, a cold or thick-bottomed glass, drafts, or candles too close together.
  • Why is the top of my candle uneven or cracked?Most often the pouring temperature. Vessel shape, fragrance amount and pouring speed also play a part. A heat gun fixes it after 24 hours.
  • What's the difference between container wax and pillar wax?Container wax melts lower (about 43–47 °C) and clings to glass. Mold (pillar) wax melts at about 57–63 °C, is harder and smoother, and holds its shape as it burns.
  • Which wax should I use for candles?The book works mainly with coconut wax, and also uses soy wax and white beeswax. It prefers natural waxes to mineral ones.
  • How much fragrance should I use in a candle?10–12% of the wax weight. For example, 19–22.8 g of fragrance in 180–185 g of coconut wax.
  • What temperatures should I use for coconut wax candles?Melt fully at about 70 °C, add fragrance at 55–60 °C, pour at 55–60 °C, and never exceed 85 °C.
  • How should I prepare and burn a new candle?Let it set 24 hours, trim the wick to 0.5 cm, cure 7–10 days, then burn 1.5–2 hours at a time until the whole surface melts, never more than 3 hours.
  • How do I attach a wick to a jar?Use a heat-rated double-sided wick sticker on a dust-free glass, not a drop of wax.
  • How do I color candle wax?Use oil-soluble ('F') or mixed ('F+P') candle dyes, crayons, mica or natural colorants, added gradually. Never use water-based paints.
  • How do I work with candle gel?Gel needs far more heat than wax: melt it over intensely boiling water to 90–105 °C (never above 110 °C) and stir with a glass rod.
